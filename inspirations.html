LOG 03 — INSPIRATIONS

What Shaped Me

What I want to build, and the stories and games that pointed me toward it.

What I Want to Build

What I actually want to build is games that leave the player feeling messed up — not for shock value, but because the story earned it. Games don't owe anyone a happy ending any more than film or TV does. In the Korean indie game 마녀의 집 (The Witch's House), you spend the whole game controlling a girl who, it turns out, died before the story even started — the witch had swapped souls with her from the beginning. In Parasite, the family doesn't get out clean; they end up scattered, wanted by police, and worse off than when it started. Neither one flinches from that, and that's the kind of impact I want to build toward.

Horror is one genre that naturally lends itself to this — fear is one of the most direct ways to make someone feel something they can't shake. But the real goal isn't "scary," it's honest. I want to weave the specific, human kinds of fear — isolation, guilt, losing control, not being believed — into the systems and mechanics themselves, and build toward endings that don't try to make things feel okay.

I also want to pull concepts from different religions and mythologies. I really like the way developers use it for lore, and sometimes using blasphemy as its own kind of twist — taking something sacred and turning it into something wrong.

Influences

Sorted by how directly each one connects to what I want to build.

Games

Buckshot Roulette

Buckshot Roulette has one mechanic and it works really well. You're just sitting across from someone, taking turns, not knowing what's going to happen. The simplicity is what makes it tense. It taught me that you don't need a complicated system to make a player feel something — you just need the right rules.

That's exactly the kind of fear I want to build with: not a jump scare, but the dread of not knowing what's coming next.

RATSHAKER

RATSHAKER is a small, weird game that somehow gets under your skin. It doesn't look expensive or polished, but that's kind of the point. It showed me that a game doesn't need a big team or a big budget to leave an impression. Sometimes the rough edges are what make it feel real.

It's proof that you don't need a big budget to unsettle someone — just a willingness to leave the rough edges in, which is the kind of impact I want to build toward.

Anime & TV

Neon Genesis Evangelion

Evangelion taught me that people can never fully understand each other, and that we all hurt each other in some way because of that. But the point isn't to avoid people because of that — it's to choose to be with them anyway, even knowing that.

As someone who grew up between American and Korean cultures, this series helped me through my own wandering phase. It also broke almost every convention its own genre had set up before it, and built its entire backbone out of religious mythology — Angels, Adam, Lilith, the rebirth of human existence. That's a big part of why I want to build with mythology myself: it's a shortcut to meaning the player already half-recognizes.

Watch on Netflix ↗

Fullmetal Alchemist

FMA introduced me to the idea of equivalent exchange — you can't get something without giving something up. The Elric brothers spend the whole series trying to fix a mistake they made, but the real lesson wasn't about alchemy at all. Always chasing something more perfect actually takes you further from what really matters.

That idea — that cost and fear are the price of change — is something I think about when I imagine games where the player has to give something up to survive, horror or otherwise.

Watch on Crunchyroll ↗

Film

Capernaum (2018)

This movie is about a kid who takes his parents to court for bringing him into the world. It sounds strange, but it makes sense once you watch it. It didn't try to make things feel okay by the end — it just showed life as it was for that kid. I sat with it for a while after it was over.

It never once tried to comfort the audience. That's the kind of honesty I want my own work to have too — no tidy ending, no easy relief.
